**Marjan Dovjak** (also **Marijan Dovjak**), painter (b. 25 March 1928, Ljubljana, Kozarje; d. 8 August 1971, Trnovski gozd, precipice near Mrzla Rupa, buried in Ljubljana). Father Ivan Dovjak, mother Franja Dovjak, née Kunstelj.

He attended the Bežigrad Gymnasium in Ljubljana. In 1946 he enrolled in the Department of Painting at the Ljubljana Academy of Fine Arts (today the Academy of Fine Arts and Design, ALUO), graduating in 1950 under France Mihelič and Gojmir Anton Kos, and completed a painting specialisation under Gabrijel Stupica in 1952. From 1953 to 1967 he taught at various schools in Ljubljana and its surroundings (1953–56 at the lower gymnasium in Logatec, 1956–58 at the lower gymnasium in Domžale, 1958–59 at the Alojz Kebe Primary School in Šentvid (now part of Ljubljana), 1959–65 at the Hinko Smrekar Primary School in Ljubljana, 1965–67 at the Rihard Jakopič Primary School in Ljubljana), after which he worked at the art department of the Pionirski dom in Ljubljana until his death. In 1956 he became a member of the Union of Slovene Fine Artists (DSLU); he served on its supervisory board in 1961 and 1970, and on its management board in 1963 and 1968. In 1958 he undertook a short study trip to Italy, and in 1961, with the help of a Moša Pijade scholarship, he spent three months training at André Lhote’s painting school in Paris. Alongside painting, he also discovered and restored frescoes. He regularly participated in the art colony in Ečka near Zrenjanin (Serbia). He helped organise art colonies in Izlake, Škofja Loka and Idrija.

He began his painting career as a colour realist and, under the influence of colour realism, developed his own stylised interpretation of everyday life, marked by bitterness (e.g., scenes of waiting rooms, train carriages). His art can be divided into several developmental stages. The first phase in the 1950s, influenced by his academy studies, is characterised by everyday scenes to which he added a personal note or conceived imaginatively. During this period he developed stylisation and flattened the composition, hinting at abstraction without achieving it. A subdued colour palette was soon replaced by strong colour contrasts. In the second phase, the influence of André Lhote’s Paris school is felt, where he encountered surrealism and cubism and himself began to paint in a post-cubist manner. He abandoned the illusion of depth and built compositions with striking colours. Following the example of analytical cubism, he first reduced the colour scale, then emphasised colourfulness with more intense colours. He treated motifs of landscape, still life and figure according to the principle of analytical decomposition and synthetic flattening of form. In the next creative phase he focused on form, calming the colour scale and rendering it in nuances. From cubist analysis, in the final phase, characterised by flatly applied black colour, he moved into synthesis. In this developmental stage, which represented the peak of his creativity, he still devoted himself to themes from everyday life, enriching the painting with colourful decorative patterns and other details (zebra-like striping). This period is characterised by the influence of expressive figurality, playfulness in compositional design, flat rendering of the objective world, transformation of proportions and the abolition of the illusion of three-dimensional space. By flattening and reshaping bodily proportions he deformed the figure, thus developing his own unique way of treating portrait depictions.

The objective world was always at the centre of his attention. Even when other painters began to paint abstractly, he depicted figurality and everyday themes. His art was socially conditioned, expressed in numerous scenes from waiting rooms, trains, kitchens, bars, through which he voiced social criticism. Besides everyday life, intimacy, anxiety and solitude or loneliness, he often emphasised the tragic nature and hopelessness of the human condition. At the painting colonies he regularly attended, he was drawn to landscape, e.g., Vojvodina landscapes.

Art critics most often treated him as a “loner” who stemmed from cubism. He is also considered one of the first pop artists in the Slovene context.

Dovjak’s oeuvre is dominated by portraits (of his mother, father, wife, friends, workers) as well as self-portraits, still lifes, genre motifs, interiors and landscapes. His works are held by, among others, the Gorenjska Museum, the Moderna galerija and ALUO. He also devoted himself to mural painting and produced several frescoes and sgraffitos (e.g., the fresco *Produkcija elektrike* and a sgraffito in the administrative building of the Elektrobosna company in Banja Luka, 1963, in collaboration with [Marijan Tršar](../people/marijan-trsar); a sgraffito on the theme of electricity production in the power plant in [Brestanica](../destinations/brestanica), 1965, also in collaboration with [Marijan Tršar](../people/marijan-trsar)).

In 1967 he received third prize at the Ex tempore in Piran, in 1971 the Župančič Award, and posthumously a purchase prize at the 10th Likovni susret in Subotica. In 1972 the plenum of art colonies in Vojvodina named the Pot Marjana Dovjaka (Marjan Dovjak Trail) after him, linking art colonies in Slovenia and Vojvodina.
