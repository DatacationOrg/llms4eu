## Biography

Alojzij Remec was born on 10 April 1886 in Trieste. His father, Valentin, was a postman, and his mother, née Rožič, was a farmer's daughter from Osek in the Vipava Valley. Remec described his youth and upbringing with his grandfather Jožef Rožič in his memoirs *Tata* (NOja 1953, 35–45).

He attended primary school in Osek and Trieste, and grammar school in Trieste (1898–1906), where his Slovene teachers were Dr. K. Glaser and Dr. Iv. Merhar; the latter had a particularly encouraging influence on him. After graduating, he entered the seminary in Gorizia, but after three years he decided to study law, completing his doctorate in Graz in 1916.

During World War I, he served as a soldier. Afterwards, he was a law clerk in Ljubljana (1919–1927) and then a lawyer in Ptuj. From December 1935 until the German occupation in 1941, he was the mayor of Ptuj and head of the district road committee. On 15 April 1941, he was arrested by the Germans and, after being held in camps at Borel and Brestanica, was exiled with his family to Serbia. He lived in Vrnjačka Banja. In July 1945, he returned to Ptuj and resumed his law practice. As a person, he was modest and good-natured, but the hardships of the camp broke his physical strength.

## Literary Work

His literary work is mainly based on the influences of "modernism," yet it is characterized by Vipava liveliness and the difficult path of his life. In grammar school, he wrote sketches for *E*, *PL*, and *Gor*; from 1903, poems for *DS*; and from 1907, also for *LZ*, *Sn*, *DP*, *MD*, and *Zora*.

**Pseudonyms:** Slavko Vilinski, Zvoran Zvoranov, Štefan Poljanec, A. R. Štefanovič.

As a university student, he edited the student anthology *Jubilej* (Ljubljana, 1912). In his poems, he used impressionistic images and reflections to delve into the spiritual and social divisions around World War I, ultimately finding a unique expression in condensed rhythm and sharpened thought. He wrote poetry until 1923.

His prose is simpler, partly influenced by domestic tales and partly by modern lyrical sketches. Works include:
- *Veliki punt* (Gor. 1909, 1910², Celje 1953³), a peasant story from the 18th century.
- *Doma in po svetu* (MD 1910), a contemporary peasant-worker story.
- *Anno Domini* (DS 1912), a romantic story from the 17th century.
- *Škrjančki* (DS 1913), a student story.
- *Naši ljudje* (1921) and *Iz moje domovine* (1922), collections of youthful sketches and short stories.
- *Andrej Košuta* (M 1922; book 1933), a novel written in his mature years.
- *Tri božične povestice* (KGMD 1923).
- *Opustošena brajda*, scenes from the beginning of World War II depicting Ptuj and its surroundings under German occupation; only the first part was completed (SV 98, 1946).

His most notable literary critiques include: *Kontrolor Škrobar* (DS 1914, 238); *Človek z bombami* (S 1920, no. 262); *Iz modernega sveta* (S 1922, no. 149).

His most successful work was in drama. The play *General Burja* remained in manuscript, as did the three-act drama *Pavla* (performed 1912, reworked as *Učiteljica Pavla* and performed in Maribor 1921). Other plays include:
- *Kirke* (DS 1922), a comedy in four acts.
- *Magda* (LZ 1924, Spl. knj. 1924; performed in Ljubljana 1924), a tragedy built on moralistic symbolism and expressionist technique (12 stations).
- *Užitkarji* (M 1923, MK 1, 1923, 1934²), a three-act peasant tragedy intended for popular stages and frequently performed.
- *Zakleti grad* (Ljubljana 1926; performed in Maribor 1925), a romantic play in five acts about lords and serfs in the Gorizia region, based on the novella *Anno Domini*.
- *Volkodlaki* (three-act) and *Talci v Kraljevem* (one-act) (Celje 1947, Zadr. knjiž. no. 24), depicting the cruelty of the German occupation in World War II.
- *Bunda* (1944), a play written in Serbian during his exile.
- *Miklavževe vragolije*, his last dramatic work, performed in Ptuj in 1951.

In his dramatic structure, Remec favored passionate encounters between individual characters, surprising revelations, and (especially in his early works) a lyrical mood. He wrote the scene *Umor* (ND 1925, no. 23) as a satire against his critics. From 1922 to 1923, he also wrote reports on the Ljubljana Drama for *S*.
